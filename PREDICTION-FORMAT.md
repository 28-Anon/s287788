# PREDICTION VIDEOS — A DIFFERENT FORMAT

The what-if format does not transfer. This is why, and what replaces it.

---

## 1. WHY IT'S A DIFFERENT PROBLEM

**A what-if video cannot be wrong.** "What if nobody needed money" is a
thought experiment — nobody can check it against reality. That's why the
format tolerates a loose claim: the worst case is a confused viewer.

**A prediction video is a falsifiable claim with your face on it.** Every
video is a bet that resolves in public. That changes three things:

1. **Credibility becomes the product.** People don't follow a "what's coming"
   channel for entertainment, they follow it because they think you're right.
   One confident miss costs more than ten forgettable videos.
2. **The escalation engine changes.** What-if escalates by *removing*
   constraints from a fantasy. A prediction escalates by *narrowing*: from a
   pressure everyone can verify, to what that pressure forces, to a specific
   dated thing you can watch for.
3. **Being wrong becomes content — if you set it up right.** More on this in §4.

---

## 2. THE ENGINE — 22 beats, 69 seconds

Same runtime, same caption rules (3–9 words, ~3s a beat, one idea each, one
location, voice running to 1:03). What changes is the spine.

| Beats | Job |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Hook** — see §3 |
| 2–5 | **The pressure.** Something true and checkable happening now |
| ~6 | **TURN 1 — "so here's what that forces"** |
| 7–9 | **The mechanism.** Why the pressure has only one place to go |
| ~10 | **TURN 2 — "and it's already started"** |
| 11–13 | **The tells.** Specific things a viewer can go and look at |
| 14 | **The prediction**, with a timeframe |
| 15 | **The falsifier** — what would prove you wrong |
| 16–21 | **Spoken close** — confidence, why it matters, make it personal |
| 22 | **The ask** — question held on screen |
| — | Outro card, 3s |

**Beat 15 is the one nobody else does and the one that builds the channel.**
"If X happens instead, I'm wrong." It costs you nothing, it's the single
strongest credibility signal available, and it pre-empts the top comment.

### What replaces "BUT WHAT IF"

Two turns, same rhythm, different job:

- **Turn 1 — the forcing move.** "So somebody has to X." Not a hypothetical: a
  constraint. The pressure exists, and here is the only door out of it.
- **Turn 2 — the receipt.** "And it already started." Move from argument to
  evidence. This is where a prediction video earns belief that a what-if video
  never needs.

---

## 3. HOOKS FOR PREDICTIONS

Different types from the what-if hooks. Write all four, test the top two.

| Type | Shape | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **The dated claim** | "By March this will have happened" | Highest stakes, highest reward. Only for calls you'd actually bet on |
| **The tell** | "When you see X, you have about six weeks" | Gives the viewer a job. Extremely shareable — people screenshot tells |
| **The already-happening** | "This started three weeks ago and nobody noticed" | Safest, because it's reporting, not forecasting |
| **The receipt** | "Six months ago I said X. Here's what happened." | Strongest hook in the genre — but you can only use it once you have a track record. Build toward it |

## 4. THE CREDIBILITY SYSTEM

This is the part that decides whether the channel lasts.

### Never state a prediction as a fact
Say the confidence out loud. Three bands is enough, and viewers respect it:

- **Likely** — the pressure is real and the mechanism is clear
- **Plausible** — could go either way, here's why I lean this way
- **Long shot** — probably won't, but the payoff if it does is enormous

A channel that says "plausible" and is sometimes wrong outlives one that says
"guaranteed" and is usually right.

### Every prediction gets a date and a falsifier
No date means it can never be scored, and an unscoreable prediction is worth
nothing. No falsifier means you'll be accused of retro-fitting, and you'll
deserve it.

### Keep a public scorecard
Hits **and** misses, dated. This feels like exposure. It is the opposite: it is
the moat. Anyone can post a confident forecast; almost nobody posts their
misses, so doing it makes you the exception people trust.

### Being wrong is a video
"I said X. It didn't happen. Here's what I got wrong." These perform, they cost
nothing to make, and they buy more credibility than a hit does. Plan for them.

### Sourcing standard
Every checkable claim traces to something a viewer could find in one search.
If a claim needs a caveat, either put the caveat in the beat or cut the claim.
The smaller true claim always beats the bigger false one — nobody can take it
off you in the comments.

## 5. USING SOMEONE ELSE'S PREDICTIONS

Worth deciding deliberately before you build on a single source:

- **You inherit their misses.** If the source is wrong, your video is wrong,
  and it's your face on it. Their track record becomes your track record.
- **Verify the track record yourself.** Prediction sites routinely advertise
  hits and quietly drop misses. Claims like "called it months before it
  happened" are marketing until you've checked the timestamps. Check whether
  the prediction was specific and dated *at the time*, or vague enough to fit
  events afterwards.
- **Credit the source on screen.** It costs three seconds, it protects you, and
  a named source makes the video more credible, not less.
- **Better: use the method, not the calls.** Take the way of reading pressure
  and apply it yourself. Then the predictions are yours, the track record is
  yours, and nobody else's misses land on you.

---

## 6. WHAT MAKES THESE ENTERTAINING

The failure mode for prediction content is sounding like a lecture. The fixes:

- **The tell is the entertainment.** People love being given something to watch
  for. "When the price of X moves, you have six weeks" turns a viewer into a
  participant.
- **Stay in one location.** Same rule as the what-ifs. A prediction video shot
  in one room with one object beats a stock-footage montage every time.
- **Concrete over abstract, always.** Not "supply chains tighten" — a shelf, a
  price sticker, a queue.
- **Make it personal in the close.** Not "this affects markets" but "this is
  the month your rent changes." Beats 16–21 exist to land it on the viewer.
- **The ask still ends it.** A question they can answer, held on screen.
